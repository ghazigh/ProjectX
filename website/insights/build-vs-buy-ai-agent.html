Build vs. buy: off-the-shelf AI tool, or custom agent?

Custom isn't always smart, and off-the-shelf isn't always cheap. Here's a clear way to decide — before you spend on the wrong one.

The most expensive AI mistakes aren't bad builds — they're building what you could have bought, or buying what you needed to build. The right call depends on a few honest questions about your problem.

Buy (off-the-shelf) when…

  • Your problem is common. Support chat, meeting notes, generic document Q&A — mature products exist and will beat a from-scratch build on cost and reliability.
  • Speed matters more than fit. You need something working this month and "good enough" is genuinely good enough.
  • It doesn't touch your core differentiator. If the workflow isn't where you win or lose, don't pour engineering into it.
  • Your data and rules are standard. No unusual integrations, compliance, or logic.

Build (custom) when…

  • The workflow is specific to you. Your process, data, and edge cases are the point — and no product models them well.
  • It's core to the business. When the workflow is a competitive advantage, owning it is worth the investment.
  • Integration is deep. The agent must live inside your systems, with your permissions, data, and controls.
  • Reliability or compliance bars are high. You need evaluation, guardrails, and auditability a generic tool won't give you — common in regulated sectors like finance.
  • Per-seat pricing stops making sense at your scale. Sometimes a build is simply cheaper over time.

The hybrid most teams should consider

It's rarely all-or-nothing. Often the right answer is to buy the commodity parts and build the differentiated thin layer on top — use existing infrastructure (models, vector stores, orchestration) and invest your custom engineering only where your problem is genuinely unique. That's usually the best cost-to-value ratio.

A quick test

If a competitor could buy the same product and get the same result, it's probably a "buy." If the value depends on your data, workflow, or reliability standards, it's probably a "build."

Don't skip the cheap step

Before committing either way, a short audit answers it for a few thousand dollars: it maps your problem to what exists, estimates a build, and tells you which path actually pays off. That's far cheaper than discovering the answer after you've bought or built the wrong thing.
